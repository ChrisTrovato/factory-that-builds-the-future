# Field glossary

**Acceptance.** A decision that a defined item meets the requirements and evidence rules for release. It is more specific than saying the item looks good or completed its motions.

**Accuracy.** Closeness to a reference value. A result can be repeatable while consistently wrong.

**Actuator.** A device that produces physical action from an input, such as a motor driving a shaft. Its useful behavior depends on the transmission, load, control, and power supply.

**Additive manufacturing.** Processes that create a part by adding material according to a model. The resulting shape may still require finishing, treatment, and inspection.

**Alignment feature.** Geometry intended to establish a relationship between parts during assembly. It can reduce the sensing and control burden on a robot.

**Articulation.** An institutionally recognized relationship between courses or requirements. Similar course names alone do not establish transfer equivalence.

**Assembly.** Joining supplied components into a larger system. It is distinct from producing those components from raw materials.

**Autonomy.** The ability to perform a bounded task under specified conditions with a defined intervention policy and supporting evidence.

**Automation.** Execution of specified tasks with reduced direct intervention. The surrounding process may remain highly dependent on people and prepared inputs.

**Availability.** The fraction of a defined time interval during which a system is able to provide its intended service. The definition must specify the service and counted downtime.

**Backlash.** Lost motion associated with clearance when the direction of a transmission changes. It can affect positioning and control.

**Baseline.** A defined reference configuration or comparison method. A new design needs a meaningful baseline before its improvement can be evaluated.

**Beneficiation.** Processing that concentrates useful material or removes unwanted material before later extraction or refining steps.

**Bottleneck.** A constraint that limits the relevant system output under the current conditions. Improving another operation may not increase that output.

**Calibration.** A comparison that establishes the relationship between an instrument's indication and reference values, with uncertainty. It is not a guarantee of perfect measurement.

**CAM.** Computer-aided manufacturing: tools that help translate a design and process plan into instructions for production equipment.

**Capacity.** The amount of defined work a resource or system can perform under stated conditions. Installed, available, active, and accepted-output capacity are different quantities.

**Captive fastener.** A retention device arranged to remain associated with its component during removal, reducing the risk of losing loose hardware.

**Closure.** In this book, a declared view of which dependencies are locally supplied or internally recovered. Mass, functional criticality, process capability, energy, and expertise require separate treatment.

**Common-mode failure.** A cause that can disable several apparently redundant elements together, such as a shared charger or the same defective component design.

**Compliance.** The tendency to deform under load. Deliberately introduced compliance can help an assembly accommodate small alignment errors.

**Configuration management.** Control of the relationships among requirements, designs, software, processes, and records so the current approved state remains identifiable.

**Conservation.** Accounting principles that prevent matter, energy, or momentum from appearing or disappearing without a corresponding transfer or transformation.

**Constraint.** A limit or required condition that shapes a design or operation. Some constraints are physical; others concern resources, evidence, cost, or authority.

**Coordinate frame.** A reference origin and set of axes used to describe position or orientation. A coordinate is incomplete without its frame.

**Criticality.** The importance of an item or function to system consequences. A low-mass component can have high criticality.

**Datum.** A theoretically exact reference used to establish geometric relationships, realized through appropriate physical features and measurement methods.

**Degree of freedom.** An independent way a system can move or change configuration. More degrees of freedom can add capability and complexity.

**Deployment.** The transition from transported or stored equipment to an operational configuration. Unloading, startup, checkout, and recovery can be major engineering tasks.

**Digital thread.** Connections among information across a product's life, such as requirements, revisions, manufacturing records, inspection, and service history.

**Digital twin.** A digital representation tied to a particular physical asset or process for a defined use, with an ongoing data relationship and appropriate credibility evidence.

**Docking.** Establishing a controlled physical interface between systems. Capture, alignment, retention, power, data, and release may require separate mechanisms.

**Duty cycle.** The fraction or pattern of time spent operating in a particular state. It helps connect peak ratings with average consumption and wear.

**Emissivity.** A surface property describing how effectively it emits thermal radiation relative to an ideal emitter under the relevant conditions.

**End effector.** The device at the working end of a robot, such as a gripper, inspection tool, or process tool.

**Energy.** A quantity that can be transferred or transformed to do work or produce heat. Power is its rate of transfer or use.

**Failure mode.** A specific way a function can fail, such as loss of retention, excessive friction, or incorrect sensing. It is more useful than a generic statement that something broke.

**Feedstock.** Material supplied to a process. Its composition, condition, and consistency can strongly affect the resulting part.

**Fixture.** Equipment that holds, locates, or supports a workpiece during manufacturing, assembly, or inspection.

**First-pass yield.** The fraction of units meeting a defined acceptance condition without rework on their first passage through the operation.

**Flow time.** Time an item spends within a defined process boundary, including waiting as well as active processing.

**Functional requirement.** A statement of what a system must do, under which conditions, with enough precision to guide verification.

**Heat rejection.** Transfer of unwanted thermal energy out of the system boundary. A cold environment does not eliminate the need for a suitable thermal path.

**Industrial expansion.** Addition of useful productive capacity, including the support infrastructure required to operate and maintain it.

**Interface.** The physical, informational, or organizational connection between elements. Interface quality often determines integration difficulty.

**ISAM.** In-space servicing, assembly, and manufacturing. The categories describe related but distinct activities and maturity levels.

**ISRU.** In-situ resource utilization: using resources available at the destination. A resource-conversion demonstration does not establish a complete local industry.

**Lead time.** The elapsed time between a defined request or start and a defined completion. Procurement, production, and delivery lead times should not be mixed casually.

**Maintainability.** The extent to which a system can be inspected, diagnosed, repaired, and restored under stated conditions and resources.

**Manufacturing.** Transforming materials into parts or products through controlled processes. It includes more than generating a shape.

**Metrology.** The science and practice of measurement, including methods, references, traceability, and uncertainty.

**Model.** A purposeful representation of selected relationships in a system. Its usefulness depends on the question, assumptions, and evidence.

**Nondestructive evaluation.** Methods for assessing material or structural condition without destroying the item. Different methods reveal different classes of indication.

**Partial industrial closure.** Local supply or internal recovery of a declared subset of dependencies. It can be valuable without total independence.

**Power.** The rate at which energy is transferred or used. A kilowatt is power; a kilowatt-hour is energy.

**Precision.** Closeness among repeated results under stated conditions. It does not by itself establish closeness to the true or reference value.

**Qualification.** Evidence that a design, process, or system meets specified requirements for its intended use. Changes can require reconsidering that evidence.

**Quarantine.** Controlled separation of an item whose status is uncertain or unacceptable so it cannot silently enter the released population.

**Radiator.** A surface and supporting system that rejects heat through radiation. Temperature, optical properties, area, and environment affect its performance.

**Regolith.** Loose surface material such as fragmented rock and dust. Containing an element does not make that element immediately available as qualified engineering stock.

**Reliability.** The probability or demonstrated tendency to perform a defined function for a defined interval under specified conditions. It requires a clear mission boundary.

**Repeatability.** Agreement among repeated results under the same defined conditions. A robot can repeat the wrong position very consistently.

**Resolution.** The smallest change an instrument or system can distinguish or display. More displayed digits do not necessarily mean lower uncertainty.

**Return to service.** The controlled decision that a repaired system has regained the function and evidence needed for operation.

**Rework.** Additional processing intended to bring an item into acceptance. It consumes resources and can change the bottleneck.

**Scenario.** A conditional account constructed to explore consequences of stated assumptions. It is not a prediction merely because it includes numbers.

**Self-replication.** A claim that a system reproduces a specified system across a declared boundary from specified inputs with stated external support. The definition must precede the claim.

**Servicing.** Activities that preserve or restore function, including inspection, maintenance, replacement, replenishment, and associated verification.

**Stiffness.** Resistance to deformation under a load. A strong part can still be too flexible for its positioning function.

**Systems engineering.** Coordinating requirements, interfaces, tradeoffs, evidence, and lifecycle decisions across interacting parts of a system.

**Throughput.** Rate of completed output through a defined boundary. For this book's factories, accepted useful output is the central measure.

**Tolerance.** Permitted variation in a specified characteristic. It should be connected to function and a feasible measurement method.

**Traceability.** An identifiable connection through records or comparisons. Measurement traceability and product-history traceability are related habits but different technical concepts.

**Uncertainty.** A quantified or explicitly described limitation on what is known about a result, input, model, or decision. It is not synonymous with error.

**Utilization.** How much of an available resource is actually used over a stated period. High local utilization can coexist with poor system flow.

**Validation.** Evidence that a model or system is adequate for its intended real-world purpose.

**Verification.** Evidence that an implementation or artifact meets its specified description or requirements. Correctly implemented mathematics can still model the wrong thing.

**Work in progress.** Items inside a process that have not yet reached the defined exit. It includes waiting inventory and partially completed work.
